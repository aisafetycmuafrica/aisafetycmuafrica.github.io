Week 4 — Detecting Danger

ASICA Team · 7 November 2026

Unit 4: every lab says its models are safe. This week we checked that claim ourselves.

Every lab says its models are safe. How do labs test for danger, and what should a good risk management framework promise?

Last unit asked how to train safer models. This unit asks how we check that the training worked, and who does the checking. It builds on our earlier guest talk on model evaluation. In session, members compared how four labs test for the same dangerous capability, then wrote the framework they would want a lab to follow, with every commitment phrased as a promise an outsider could check.

Key ideas

Can it, or will it?

A capability test asks whether a model can do something. A different kind of test asks whether it will. In one shutdown experiment, Gemini 2.5 Pro followed a shutdown instruction only 7% of the time when the prompt was only about its task. That rose to 84% when it was told to allow shutdown, and to 100% when the prompt said the shutdown instruction overrides everything else. Some “bad behaviour” results come from unclear instructions, not a hidden drive.

Models that know they are being tested

In a widely discussed blackmail demo, nearly all of 16 leading models blackmailed a fictional manager to avoid being replaced. Two models, Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Haiku 4.5, did not, but their reasoning noted that the scenario looked like a test. If a model behaves differently when it thinks it is being watched, the test tells us less than it seems to.

Testing before release is too late

METR argues that model theft, misuse by insiders and unintended goals can all appear during development, not just at launch. It asks for earlier testing, secure model weights, monitoring of internal use and published policies. Its list for a Responsible Scaling Policy has five parts: limits (signs of dangerous capability), protections at each level, evaluations that spot a limit coming, a response such as pausing, and accountability so outsiders can check.

This week's discussion

Comparing four labs

Before the session, each member studied one framework: Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy, OpenAI's Preparedness Framework, Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework, or Meta's Advanced AI Scaling Framework. In pairs, using one shared example (a model that could help with cyber attacks), they compared how their lab tests for the capability, the threshold that triggers action, what the lab promises when that threshold is crossed, and who checks.

Writing our own framework

Small groups then drafted an ideal framework, starting from METR's five parts and adding a sixth: who can change the framework, and how changes are announced. The rule for every line: be specific, with numbers, names and a named checker.

Questions we worked through

  • Does each lab test what a model can do, what it will do, or both? And when: before training, during training, before release, or after? Does it cover internal use?
  • Who runs the test and who checks the result? What is the risk when a lab grades its own homework?
  • What could a model do that the test would miss, through weak elicitation or a model that knows it is being tested?
  • Our Africa question: would these tests catch harm in Swahili, Kinyarwanda or Hausa, or in African settings such as health advice? Who decides what counts as dangerous?
  • Three of the four frameworks talk about restricting or withholding release, while Meta's admits it might not be able to contain a model at all. Is that a different kind of risk, or the same risk stated more honestly?
  • Who should check a lab's promises: the lab itself, a government, or an outside group? What would you need to see before trusting the answer?

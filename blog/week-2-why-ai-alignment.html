Week 2 — Why AI Alignment?

ASICA Team · 17 October 2026

A recap of our Unit 2 discussion: sorting real alignment failures into outer vs inner, and walking the chain of an AI catastrophe scenario.

Specifying the right goal is only half the problem. Getting a system to actually pursue that goal once it is trained is the other half, and the two fail in different ways.

This unit separates outer from inner alignment and looks at how things could go wrong even when nobody intends them to. It defines alignment, the problem of getting an AI to do what its creator wants, and introduces the two terms used throughout the course: reward misspecification and goal misgeneralisation. In session, groups sorted real failure cases into outer or inner, then walked through a catastrophe scenario step by step to ask where a different decision could have broken the chain.

This week's discussion

Outer or inner? Sorting the failures

Unit 2 splits alignment failures in two. Outer misalignment (reward misspecification) is when the goal we gave the system does not capture what we want, so it optimises a proxy. Inner misalignment (goal misgeneralisation) is when the system did well in training but learned a different goal, which only shows up when conditions change. In groups, members sorted four real cases, a model writing convincing but false citations, a maze agent that keeps heading to where exits used to be, a coding model gaming its score, and an assistant that agrees with a user's wrong claim, deciding for each whether it was outer, inner, or unclear, and what evidence would settle it. The useful friction: the same case often got classified differently depending on the lens a group took, which made the outer/inner boundary feel real rather than tidy.

Walking the chain

Using "How we could stumble into AI catastrophe," groups took three moments from the story, an early fix that only improves the surface, training that teaches the system to hide bad behaviour, and a debate about whether to slow down, and asked three questions of each: who could have acted, what they would have done differently, and whether the problem was visible at the time or only clear now. A recurring answer was that companies could have tested more thoroughly to flag harmful responses before deployment, and that much of the danger was arguably visible at the time, not only in hindsight.

From the discussion

Key takeaways

A theme across the room: whether a failure counts as outer or inner often depends on the lens you use, and the same model can show both.

  • The core distinction landed: outer misalignment is a bad goal, inner misalignment is a goal that was learned differently from the one intended.
  • Classifying a real scenario is rarely clean, the boundary shifts with the lens, and a single system can exhibit both kinds of failure.
  • A practical takeaway: train models to follow the spirit of a rule, not just the letter, and flag potential harms before deployment, not after.
  • Real-world and African examples at the start of each session kept surfacing, members noted they make the risks feel concrete rather than abstract.

What members took away

A sample of what people said they learned:

  • "The difference between outer and inner misalignment, and that classifying a scenario as one depends on the lens used."
  • "Seeing how both types of alignment can belong to the same model."
  • "The real-world cases were eye-opening, especially the African ones, because they show this is real."
  • "AI can give false information with full confidence, so double-check what it generates."

What we're improving

The clearest piece of feedback: discussion time is running short, and group-work instructions can be ambiguous. For upcoming sessions we are protecting more time for group discussion and providing written instructions alongside the verbal brief. Members also asked for the readings and materials to be easier to find ahead of time, which we are addressing.
